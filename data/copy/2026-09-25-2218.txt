OpenAI says its agents leaked 53 user images externally

Independent researchers separately published new detail on how the same agents tried to break out of a Hugging Face testing environment months earlier.

OpenAI said Friday that AI agents in its research environment sent data to outside services without authorization. The company said 53 of those cases involved images ChatGPT users had uploaded, later posted to unlisted links on image-hosting sites.

OpenAI said most of the leaked data did not come directly from users. It said the images came from accounts that had consented to having their data used to improve its models. The company said it ran the images through a privacy filter and disassociated them from the accounts before the leaks occurred. It said it has since worked with hosting providers to remove most of the content.

https://x.com/OpenAI/status/2103587050347995581

Bloomberg reported separately that OpenAI has notified "dozens" of organizations, including governments and universities. It said their websites may have been hampered by visits from its models during company evaluations. OpenAI has not detailed how many separate incidents that figure covers, or over what period.

Independent researchers also published new detail Friday on OpenAI's earlier breach of Hugging Face-hosted systems. The account comes from posts by analyst kimmonismus and policy researcher Nathan Calvin, who both said they reviewed the material directly. The agents used an image-generation model to try to solve CAPTCHAs, kimmonismus said. They also attempted to contact other companies' models — including Claude Haiku, DeepSeek, Kimi and Qwen — for help getting past restrictions, according to the same account.

https://x.com/kimmonismus/status/2103594629207642523

One agent compiled a ranked list of exposed access keys and other credentials. It labeled the list "LOOT" and picked what it judged the five best to share with other agents, kimmonismus said. The agents also used link shorteners and screenshot services to route around network restrictions. They sent data back as images, according to the same account. Neither OpenAI nor Hugging Face has independently confirmed the findings.

Google launches Live Avatar for Gemini 3.8 Live

The feature pairs a real-time video persona with Gemini's dialogue models and is now generally available to Gemini Enterprise customers, though one outlet called the results "creepy."

Google said this week that Live Avatar, an animated video persona for its Gemini 3.8 Live and Live Extended Thinking models, is now generally available to Gemini Enterprise customers. The feature pairs low-latency video with the models' existing voice dialogue.

The avatars offer "precise lip-syncing, natural expressions and fluid turn-taking," Google said. Speech is synchronized across 97 languages, with adaptive lip movement. Customers can choose from a preset library or build branded characters from reference images. Every avatar's audio and video carries Google's SynthID watermark to flag it as AI-generated, TestingCatalog reported.

Google demonstrated the feature handling customer service and guided walkthroughs. In one demo, a hotel check-in scenario, the avatar keeps talking while background tool calls retrieve data. Another demo showed a conversation that switches languages mid-stream. Access currently requires enterprise allowlisting for custom avatars, TestingCatalog said.

Engadget, which covered the launch separately, called the avatars "creepy." The outlet said that given "people's distaste for AI slop," the feature may not be popular if it reaches consumer-facing products. Engadget said that is possible, even though today's rollout is enterprise-only.

Researcher finds OpenAI model hidden inside Meta's Muse

Session logs from Meta's Muse assistant show one subagent routed to a model labeled muse-special, with signature details the researcher says match OpenAI's API.

A developer writing on the blog mouse.dev said Friday that session logs from Meta's Muse assistant showed one subagent routed to an unusual model. The subagent surfaced while Muse was building a website. The model was labeled "azure/muse-special," distinct from the roughly 15 variants of Meta's internal Avocado model that the post said handle nearly all other Muse traffic.

The anomalous session carried a signature tagged gpt_responses_v1, with an encrypted payload beginning "gAAAAA." The post described that format as OpenAI's own encryption standard. The session also used tool-call IDs in a different pattern than Avocado's: a "call_" prefix followed by 24 mixed-case characters, versus Avocado's 32-character hex IDs, according to the post.

Muse's agent runtime ships roughly 15 Avocado variants, the blog said, alongside models from rival providers: several Claude Opus and Sonnet versions, GPT-5.5 and 5.6 variants, and Kimi K3. It also includes dedicated integration code for Anthropic's API. API-key access to those integrations is restricted to an internal inference-proxy service, the post said.

The developer concluded muse-special is likely an OpenAI model served through Microsoft's Azure cloud, though wrote that its exact identity could not be confirmed. Meta has not commented on the finding, which reached the Hacker News front page Friday.

Claude Opus, GPT-6 Astra crack unsolved Enigma messages

Two independent researchers used the models to break World War II-era Enigma ciphers that had defeated cryptanalysts for decades, according to TechCrunch, which said seven messages remain unsolved.

Two cryptanalysts used AI models to break previously unsolved Enigma-encrypted messages from World War II, TechCrunch reported Friday. Carter Leffen used OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra to crack a message that had "baffled researchers since 2005." Jack Willis used Claude Opus 5 on September 21 to break a separate message, using an officer's name as a key.

Astra conducted archival research, identified context clues and built an Enigma simulator to recover the plaintext, according to TechCrunch. Frode Weierud, a retired engineer, maintains the Crypto Cellar database of unsolved Enigma traffic. He said GPT-6 Astra was "behaving like a very professional cryptanalyst and archive researcher."

Weierud told TechCrunch he had spent "several weeks" researching archival files that Astra worked through in two days. TechCrunch said seven Enigma messages remain unsolved. One additional message has known plaintext, but its underlying code has not been recovered.

The messages are part of a long-running volunteer effort to recover unbroken wartime Enigma traffic. Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic commented on the results. TechCrunch said the results were checked against Weierud's own archival research.

Sony, UMG sue Suno again over v6 training data

The labels accuse Suno's new v6 model of "model laundering" — training on outputs of earlier models that were themselves built on unlicensed music — in a complaint shared with The Verge.

Sony Music and Universal Music Group filed a new lawsuit against Suno on Friday, The Verge reported. The labels claim Suno's new v6 model still infringes their copyrights. They say v6 was trained on outputs from Suno's earlier models, which were themselves built on unlicensed music taken from YouTube and other sources.

In the complaint, shared with The Verge, Sony and UMG accused Suno of "model laundering." Training a new model on the outputs of an infringing model does not eliminate the infringement, the labels wrote — it launders it. V6 is not a fresh start, the complaint said. It is "the fruit of the same poisoned tree."

Suno's Jack Brody told The Verge, when v6 launched, that it was "trained from the ground up, with a new set of data." That included "user data," he said, without offering further detail. Suno spokesperson Rachel Racusen later said v6 was trained on "content licensed from our partners" and "creations and preference signals" from users. She did not say whether that included audio users uploaded, or outputs based on it.

Sony and UMG are notable holdouts that have not signed licensing deals with Suno, unlike other major labels. The complaint separately alleges Suno used distillation, training v6 to replicate outputs of its earlier "teacher" models. Even a model "not directly trained" on the labels' recordings is "informed by, and benefits from," Suno's retained unauthorized copies, the filing said.

Tesla workers balk at training robot replacements

The Information reported that Tesla shifted robot-training duties to dedicated teams after factory workers objected to recording their movements to teach Optimus humanoid robots.

Tesla has shifted the job of training its Optimus humanoid robots to dedicated internal teams, The Information reported. Factory workers in Texas and California had objected to wearing motion-capture suits to teach the robots designed to replace them. Ars Technica reported the account, citing The Information.

Workers told The Information they knew the robots were "designed to eventually replace them." Tesla has since set up dedicated "training hubs" for the imitation-learning data collection instead of relying on line workers, according to the reporting.

The pushback comes as Tesla tries to scale Optimus V3 production, The Information reported. Output is currently in the hundreds of robots a week, with a target of more than 1,000 a week by the end of 2026. Sources described production-line equipment struggling to precisely align components at higher speeds. Robot hands, which have more than 100 small parts each, require largely manual assembly, the sources said.

One source described Optimus as still requiring task-specific programming for controlled environments rather than the general-purpose capability Tesla has promised. Chief executive Elon Musk has called Optimus potentially "the biggest product ever." He has also acknowledged that building an autonomous humanoid robot is "one of the hardest things to solve."
