## Google is testing a Gemini 4 checkpoint called Carbon, Business Insider reports

**Business Insider reports a staffer says Carbon "feels like Opus 5.5" for coding, weeks after Google showed Argon to select cyber partners only.**

Google is internally testing a newer Gemini 4 checkpoint codenamed Carbon, according to a Business Insider report relayed on X on Friday by the analyst accounts AndrewCurran_ and kimmonismus, and by synthwavedd. Business Insider's page could not be opened for this story, so the details below come from those relays and from Techmeme's summary of the article. Google has not commented in anything seen here.

https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2108651657915732034

The relayed account says Carbon matches Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 on coding. Techmeme's summary of the report quotes one staffer as saying the checkpoint "feels like Opus 5.5". Synthwavedd, citing the same employee, wrote that the person said they "would need to test it more", so the comparison is an impression from one tester, not a measured result.

Carbon follows Argon, the top model of the Gemini 4 generation. Reuters reported on 1 October that Google announced Argon the day before, giving it only to select cybersecurity partners and setting no public release date. A Google spokesperson called it the "most performant model yet built for complex workloads", and the company's own benchmark table has Argon trailing on two of four coding tests.

That context explains why a coding comparison matters. Reuters also reported that Google dropped plans for Gemini 3.5 Pro and reshuffled DeepMind leadership, leaving it behind Anthropic and OpenAI. Kimmonismus argued in a post that Google now looks competitive again. That is the poster's opinion, and nothing in the reporting shows how Carbon compares with Argon.

What is not known is large. No benchmark numbers for Carbon have been published, nobody outside Google has tested it, and it is unclear whether Carbon will ship under that name or fold into Argon. The Business Insider report appears to be the only outlet with the story so far. The paper has not confirmed it with Google.

## Microsoft releases Decision-1, a Qwen-based model that scores options

**Microsoft says Decision-1 beat every model it tried on 36 benchmarks, runs 35 times faster than GPT-6 Sol, and charges $0.042 per million input tokens with free output.**

Microsoft has released Microsoft-Decision-1, a model that does not write prose. Given a fixed set of options, it returns a calibrated probability for each. The company announced it on 9 October in a post by Achint Srivastava, a vice president in the Office of the CTO, and it is available now in Microsoft Foundry. Microsoft says OpenRouter access is coming soon.

Microsoft says the model is Qwen3.5-9B, the open-weight model from Alibaba, post-trained for single-pass decision scoring. It handles yes/no, multiple-choice and rating questions, plus rubric grading of AI responses and agent actions. Microsoft pitches it at routing, triage or verification, where software needs a structured answer it can act on straight away.

https://x.com/testingcatalog/status/2108647491033821668

The headline numbers are Microsoft's own claims and have not been independently checked. It says Decision-1 reached the highest accuracy in a 36-benchmark comparison of nearly 150,000 questions kept blind from training. At median latency it says the model is 4.5 times quicker than the runner-up, Quyet-1.0-Large, and 35 times quicker than GPT-6 Sol.

Microsoft also reports stability figures. It says the model changes its decision on 1.3 per cent of perturbed inputs on average, with no flips when option descriptions are paraphrased or reordered. Internally, it says Xbox Research labelled more than 10,000 pieces of feedback at quality competitive with GPT-6 Sol, at over 14 times the speed and 200 times lower cost.

Pricing is $0.042 per million input tokens, and output tokens are free, Microsoft says. It also plans to rebase the model on other systems, including Microsoft AI and OpenAI models. The release lands the same day TechCrunch reported that TypeSafe AI, maker of the similar non-text model Jev, raised $870 million. Independent tests of Decision-1 have not been published.

## Developer says Claude Code and Jev found a lost meteorite in old archives

**Jesse Waites says a home GPU and about $3 of Jev queries sifted 4.35 million Dutch East India Company pages overnight, and the findings await specialist review.**

Developer Jesse Waites says he pointed AI tools at centuries of digitised archives and found a forgotten meteorite fall, three live rhinos shipped in the 1730s and three eruptions missing from the Smithsonian's list. His write-up, dated 8 October, reached the Hacker News front page with 74 points. The findings are his own, and he says they await specialist review.

Waites says Claude Code built the pipeline, with a GPU in a home lab handling embedding and search. A small, fast model, Jev, acted as a filter for narrow yes/no questions, and Claude Haiku then handled reading, translation and detail extraction for flagged passages. A second Claude Code agent checked transcriptions against scans and catalogues. He says the overnight Dutch East India Company run took twelve hours on one GPU.

The sources were GLOBALISE transcriptions of the company's records, about 4.35 million pages, plus the Dutch national library's Delpher newspapers, two centuries of American newspapers and some ship logbooks. Waites gives no total cost, but says Jev reading 59,000 mentions of elephants cost about three dollars. That detail is his, not independently verified.

The meteorite is the headline find. Waites says an account in a Java newspaper of December 1812 describes a stone falling near Cokurrgaum, in what is now Maharashtra, on 6 August 1812. If a specialist confirms it, he says, it would be the earliest recorded meteorite fall in Maharashtra, 26 years before the current record.

The rhinos were three live Javan animals meant as gifts for the King of Kandy between 1738 and 1740. One died in 1738, he says, and two died aboard the Loverendaal in 1740. He also reports unrecorded eruptions at Gamkonora in 1722, Ciremai in 1712 and Slamet in 1780. He could not locate a 1808 or 1809 eruption he went looking for. None of this has been checked by historians.
