## Free stealth model Space Bunny Alpha draws coders, maker unnamed

**OpenRouter lists the model with a 1M-token context window and no charge, and says the provider behind it has chosen to stay anonymous.**

A model called Space Bunny Alpha appeared on OpenRouter on Tuesday at no charge, and builders began posting what they had made with it through Wednesday and Thursday. OpenRouter's listing gives it a one-million-token context window, a maximum of 524,288 completion tokens, and text, image and video input. It carries no price for prompt or completion tokens.

OpenRouter describes it as "an anonymous large model with blazing-fast inference, strong coding capabilities and native multimodal input support", and says the provider operating it has chosen to remain anonymous during the preview. That description is the provider's own. Nobody outside it has published a benchmark run, and the listing shows the model is not moderated.

The account mark_k said on Thursday that community fingerprinting points to a MiniMax M3.1 preview, and added that nobody has officially confirmed who made it. The post had 71 likes when read at 18:00 UTC on Thursday. MiniMax has not said anything publicly about the model, and OpenRouter's page names no lab.

The demos are what is carrying it. Atul Kumar, who has 141,000 followers, said OpenCode had made Space Bunny free for a week and that he used it to build a portfolio site with code-generated animations; the post drew 98 likes and 43,600 views. Vox, an account that posts model tests, said it produced a 1,564-line GPU fluid simulation in a single HTML file with no libraries.

Both are single accounts reporting their own results, and neither has published the prompts or the code for anyone else to check. The account polydao put first-token latency at about 1.14 seconds in its own runs. OpenRouter's listing says the model requires reasoning, with effort settings from low through max.

Stealth listings of this kind usually end with a lab claiming the model or the endpoint going dark. Neither has happened yet.

## Odyssey opens Agora-2 preview, says 20 can share one generated world

**The research lab says its multi-agent world model generates a shared environment for up to 20 humans and agents at once, with no game engine underneath.**

Odyssey has opened a playable research preview of Agora-2, a world model the lab says lets up to 20 human and agent participants act inside the same generated environment in real time. The preview drew a run of posts on Thursday afternoon, three days after Odyssey published the model's page on 21 September.

Odyssey describes Agora-2 as "a learned game engine", and says it holds an explicit shared state across participants rather than rendering each player a separate video. Every claim about how it works is the lab's own. Odyssey has published no benchmark, no weights and no paper, and no outside group has tested the preview.

The lab puts the capacity at five times that of Agora-1, its previous model, and says Agora-2 can simulate multiple environments rather than one, with longer-horizon behaviour. It says the model uses attention to predict how participants' combined actions change the shared state, and flow matching to train the renderer that draws each participant's view.

The account Documenting AGI, which had 368 likes and 27,464 views on its post by 18:00 UTC on Thursday, said the preview generates shared environments across multiple worlds with no traditional game engine under the hood. The account 0xHvdes said the generated world looks like Diablo II. Both are describing the demo rather than a controlled test.

Odyssey says it intends the worlds for training robots, autonomous vehicles and cybersecurity agents, and for studying how agents behave together before they are deployed. The preview is at agora.odyssey.ml, which redirects to the lab's own domain.

## Perplexity brings local agent to Windows PCs on AMD Ryzen AI Max

**The company says Portable Computer now runs on AMD's Ryzen AI Max series, after launching on Nvidia RTX cards earlier this year.**

Perplexity said on Thursday that Portable Computer, its local agent runtime, is now available for Windows on AMD Ryzen AI Max Series processors. The company said the agent works with a user's connected apps and local files, and can run scheduled tasks entirely on the device.

That is Perplexity's account of its own product. The company published no benchmark alongside the post, and no independent test of the AMD build has appeared. Aravind Srinivas, Perplexity's chief executive, posted separately to welcome AMD to what he called the Perplexity Computer local AI ecosystem.

The newsletter AlphaSignal, which covered the release on Thursday, said the local stack runs a 27-billion-parameter model — either Perplexity's own PPLX 27B or a Qwen 27B — behind an agent harness, planner and tool router. It put the hardware requirement at 24GB of memory addressable by the integrated GPU, matching the bar Perplexity set for Nvidia RTX cards.

AlphaSignal said the feature is for Pro and Max subscribers, that local runs do not draw on Perplexity's cloud credits, and that escalation to hosted frontier models is gated behind explicit permission. Neither Perplexity nor AMD has confirmed those details publicly, and the machines Perplexity lists as compatible are not all shipping in the same configuration.

Perplexity's connectors for the local agent cover Gmail, Outlook, Slack, GitHub, Google Drive, OneDrive and Word alongside local files, according to AlphaSignal. Perplexity did not say how many machines meet the memory bar.

## Google and Wiz point AI scanners at hospitals, rail operator

**The two say a programme called Scan for Good found an exposed rail production database and a hospital alert system, using Gemini and a pentesting agent.**

Google and its cloud security arm Wiz launched a programme on Thursday that runs AI scanners against public-facing systems at hospitals, public services and infrastructure operators. The two call it Scan for Good. It pairs Google's Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber model with Red Agent, Wiz's penetration-testing agent, and human researchers who check what the agents find.

The Register reported the launch. It said the agents had found a public rail operator's production database with active administrator sessions exposed. Wiz says that could have let an attacker take over routes, schedules and service announcements. The same report describes a hospital missing access controls, which exposed staff contact details and access to a mobile alert system.

Those are Wiz's accounts of its own findings. Gal Nagli, Wiz's head of offensive security, told The Register the programme has run for several months. The launch scales it globally, he said, with no set end date. Wiz says humans validated the impact of each finding, and notified the affected organisations privately.

One municipality had data on about 5,000 elderly residents exposed, according to the report. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency has endorsed the programme. Nick Andersen, its acting director, said defensive vulnerability discovery helps strengthen the nation's digital infrastructure. CISA's approval does not make the scanning mandatory.

Organisations apply through Wiz's website. Others are covered by bug bounty programmes and disclosure policies they already run. Neither Google nor Wiz has published how many systems the agents scanned, or how many findings turned out to be false positives.

## Canonical moves Ubuntu to weekly kernels, cites AI bug discovery

**The company says language models and specialised agents have turned bug hunting into an automated engine, and that CVE volume has grown past its old release cycle.**

Canonical is replacing Ubuntu's kernel release cadence with overlapping two-week cycles that put out a kernel every week. The company says the change answers the number of vulnerabilities now being found. It set out the new strategy in a post on 23 September.

Canonical puts part of the growth down to automation. "Large language models (LLMs) and specialized AI agents have transformed bug discovery from a manual, time-intensive process into a highly automated engine," it wrote. The volume of CVEs "has skyrocketed exponentially", it said. The company gave no figure for how many kernel CVEs it now handles.

The second cause it names is procedural. The upstream Linux kernel community became a CVE Numbering Authority in 2024. It began issuing identifiers for thousands of bugs that had gone untracked, on the view that almost any kernel flaw on a running system may have security consequences.

Ubuntu used to ship a full kernel update every four weeks, with a security update two weeks later. The replacement runs a two-week cycle that starts a week apart. Week one is integration, builds and smoke tests, ending with release candidates in the -proposed pocket. Week two is certification and regression testing.

Teams that cannot wait can test the -proposed builds themselves and skip certification. Those builds refresh weekly. Canonical said it aims to get environments "into a defensible, safer state within 24 to 48 hours of public disclosure", with workarounds where a patch is not ready. The Register covered the change on Thursday.

## NSA tells lawmakers it spends billions testing AI models this year

**The Washington Sun reports the figure from two people familiar with classified estimates, and says proposals for a civilian AI regulator are costed far lower.**

The National Security Agency has told lawmakers it is spending billions of dollars this year to test and evaluate advanced AI models, The Washington Sun reported on Thursday, citing two people familiar with classified intelligence estimates. No other outlet has the figure, and the agency has not stated an amount publicly.

The comparison the piece draws is with the sums Congress has attached to civilian oversight. It puts the cost of the AI Security and Innovation Act at roughly $20 million a year, and a separate House bill on AI reporting at $36 million over five years. The reporting does not say how the NSA's classified spending is divided between models, staff and compute.

A Defense Department spokesperson declined to give a number. "For security reasons, the Department does not discuss the technical architecture or resource allocation for its AI tools," the spokesperson said, according to the report.

Nathan Calvin, general counsel at the advocacy group Encode, told the paper that in-house evaluation capability is "important and necessary" but "genuinely expensive". The report also quotes Nat Purser of the AI Verification and Evaluation Research Institute. Neither is in a position to confirm the classified figure.

The piece estimates that a full government AI regulatory system could run to tens of billions of dollars a year. That is the reporter's projection rather than a costing published by any agency, and no bill before Congress proposes a body at that scale.

## CRISPR researcher calls Anthropic enzyme find routine genome mining

**Lucas Harrington of Mammoth Biosciences says the search method is decades old and that similar systems have been known since 2008.**

A named CRISPR researcher has disputed the framing of Anthropic's claim, reported here on Tuesday, that Claude agents found a previously uncharacterised enzyme system in DNA sequence databases. Lucas Harrington, a co-founder of Mammoth Biosciences who trained in Jennifer Doudna's laboratory, said the method behind the search has been around for decades.

Harrington said similar systems have been known since 2008, according to The Decoder, which reported his comments on Thursday. "The hard part is figuring out what a system actually does," he said, and presenting early results as a major discovery "isn't helpful". The Decoder is the only outlet carrying his remarks, and Anthropic has not responded to them publicly.

What the system does remains the open question. Anthropic said the array-associated reverse transcriptases its agents surfaced pair a repeating DNA pattern with a reverse transcriptase gene, and called that arrangement reminiscent of CRISPR. The company also said its work on the primary function was still ongoing, and no other group has reported reproducing the result.

Feng Zhang of MIT and the Broad Institute, the outside researcher Anthropic cited on Tuesday, called the finding intriguing and worth further investigation without saying it was confirmed. Harrington's objection is narrower and different in kind: not that the result is wrong, but that database mining of this sort is ordinary work.

TechCrunch, writing on Tuesday, took a different detail as the notable one — that Anthropic has not let Claude run unsupervised in its laboratory, and that humans remain in the loop. Anthropic's post reached 748 points and 761 comments on Hacker News by Thursday evening.
