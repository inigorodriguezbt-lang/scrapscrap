## Mathematicians split over OpenAI's 700-paper release, The Decoder reports

**OpenAI put more than 700 manuscripts claiming solutions to open problems on GitHub on 6 October, and over 100 mathematicians have since answered in public.**

OpenAI published more than 700 manuscripts on GitHub on 6 October, claiming solutions to hundreds of open mathematics problems, The Decoder reported on Saturday. The outlet did not give an exact count of problems or proofs. The math blog Proofs and Prompts has since collected more than 100 reactions from researchers, which The Decoder summarised. Nobody outside OpenAI has yet verified the results.

The Decoder says it is too early to assess the mathematics. Some papers have been withdrawn, and others are criticised as hard to read. Peter Scholze, a Fields medallist, urged patience and said human understanding takes time. He also warned that such systems might one day be able to break widely used encryption, according to the report.

Terence Tao was mixed, The Decoder says. He saw clever new ideas but was deeply frustrated by the lack of people to discuss, present or teach the work. Alvaro Lozano-Robledo called 6 October possibly the most important day in the field's history, while noting that OpenAI aimed at the Riemann Hypothesis and reached only a weaker variant.

Several mathematicians described personal stakes. Ben Green said the release resolved roughly three-quarters of the goals of a European grant he won in June. Matt Zaremsky said OpenAI took over a problem he had worked on for four years, calling the experience not great. A PhD student, Tristan Humbert, said OpenAI claimed the problem at the centre of his dissertation.

Others pushed back on how the work was released. Henry Wilton criticised the withholding of author names and of any failure-rate information. Enrico Fatighenti argued that AI papers face a lower standard than human work, leaving the community to decode them. The Association for Human Mathematics has called for a boycott of OpenAI, The Decoder says. Only one outlet's account has been checked here, and OpenAI's own wording was not reviewed.

## Indie studio takes bank loan to keep voice-AI driving demo running

**Easy Fox says its free Steam demo of Teach My Little Sister How To Drive costs over $1,000 a day in AI fees after players grew twentyfold.**

Easy Fox, a four-person studio, says the free Steam demo of its game Teach My Little Sister How To Drive now costs more than $1,000 a day in AI services. Tom's Hardware reported on Saturday that the team took out a bank loan to keep it running. The studio's own Steam announcement, dated 7 October, says demo players grew more than twentyfold in a month.

In the game you sit in the passenger seat and steer your sister, who is driving on dangerous roads, with your voice alone. The Steam page describes it as a chaotic simulation. Each spoken command is processed by an AI model, so every play session consumes paid tokens, which explains why a popular free demo becomes expensive.

According to the studio's announcement, Google's Gemini has been returning usage-limit errors, so the game leans more on a fallback OpenAI model. Players have complained that the fallback gives long and unnatural replies. Tom's Hardware reports the studio is considering local models to escape cloud limits, though no decision has been confirmed.

Easy Fox says it may close the demo earlier than planned but has not decided to. For the paid release, the studio says it will build expected AI usage into the price, so players do not pay for tokens separately. The $1,000 figure is the studio's own, with no breakdown of costs or accounting period given in the coverage seen.
