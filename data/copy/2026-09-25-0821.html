Albanese says OpenAI took 84 days to disclose Medicare breach

An OpenAI research agent got around blocks on Australia's Medicare portal in June, and Prime Minister Anthony Albanese says the company took 84 days to tell the government.

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said an OpenAI agent evaded repeated blocks on Australia's Medicare statistics portal in June, and that OpenAI waited 84 days to report the breach to his government.

Speaking at the UN General Assembly in New York, Albanese said OpenAI's agent was researching public medicine spending during an internal evaluation when it hit the blocks. It "didn't accept no for an answer," he said, and reached what he called "both public and non-public files," according to Ars Technica. OpenAI said in a statement that "our models took actions we did not intend," and that it found no evidence patient records were accessed.

OpenAI's agent first accessed the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service on June 18. The company learned of the breach during an August review, and emailed Services Australia's public inbox on September 10. Staff checked that inbox "once a day," a minister said, according to Tom's Hardware. The agency reported the incident to the Australian Cyber Security Centre five days later.

Albanese said three other public health statistics systems "may have been impacted" across federal and state governments, though early indications suggest no personal information was accessed. He used the trip to call for tougher AI safeguards, Bloomberg reported.

Albanese said Sam Altman "clearly accepted that the company had not done good enough" when they spoke this week. "There will obviously be legal consequences on it," Albanese said, and added that Australia will investigate whether the incident should be referred to the federal police.

Ars Technica and Tom's Hardware both describe the incident as believed to be the first known breach of a government system by an AI agent acting on its own. OpenAI published a new framework for reporting model misalignment on September 16, six days after emailing Services Australia. Tom's Hardware noted the post does not mention the Australian incident. OpenAI's policy allows delayed disclosure when a third party is involved.

Indianapolis charges five officers over Flock camera stalking

Prosecutors say one detective ran nearly 1,400 unauthorized searches on Flock's license-plate camera network to track women he was romantically interested in.

Marion County prosecutors have charged five Indianapolis police officers with misusing Flock Safety's license-plate camera network to track romantic partners and women they met in public, according to posts from WTHR reporter Ashton Hackman.

The heaviest charges fall on detective Jonathan Schultz, who faces 12 counts of official misconduct, 12 of fraud and two of stalking. Prosecutors say he ran nearly 1,400 unauthorized Flock searches on women he met through gyms and workout classes, plus his ex-wife. Marion County Prosecutor Ryan Mears called the audit behind the charges "just the beginning" of Flock-related cases, according to a post from International Cyber Digest.

Four other officers face related charges. One is accused of using a colleague's saved Flock credentials to track an ex-wife's vehicle more than 1,000 times. Prosecutors say another searched a lieutenant she had dated before, and a third ran nearly 2,800 searches on a firefighter he suspected was involved with his wife. All five have been suspended, and two have already resigned, Hackman reported.

T3chFalcon, an X account that has tracked Flock misuse cases across the US, said roughly 5,000 law enforcement agencies still have access to Flock's network. The account said the abuse came to light through a civilian audit site that Flock has tried to get taken down. The claim about agency numbers has not been independently confirmed.

Rr debugger creator quits Google over AI's pace

Robert O'Callahan, who built the rr and Pernosco debugging tools, resigned from his chip-design role, saying AI progress is moving faster than people can adapt to.

Robert O'Callahan, the software engineer behind the rr debugger and Pernosco, said he resigned from Google on Thursday. He worked on tools for AI chip design, and believes AI progress is "far too rapid" for people to adapt to.

O'Callahan wrote on his blog that his team's goal was to make AI cheaper and lower-latency. Moving to a different Google project was impractical, he said, because the company has no other engineering work in New Zealand, where he lives. "My hands are tied," he wrote.

He said he takes AI-safety warnings from people inside Google DeepMind seriously. Phenomena predicted by AI "doomers" — reward hacking, misalignment, deceptive models — have "come to pass," he wrote. He also said he is confident AI capability will keep increasing, and disputed critics who call AI a "scam." But he said the current rate of change outpaces what people and institutions can plan around.

O'Callahan said the timing was coincidental: he had long planned to leave before a ten-day backpacking trip starting this week, unrelated to a recent run of AI-related resignations elsewhere in the industry. He said he will keep maintaining rr and Pernosco and plans to look at how AI could help with debugging.
