## Microsoft benchmark finds agents often claim success but leave databases wrong

**Microsoft's ThinkingBox checks what an AI agent actually changed in a database, not what it said. Across 121,680 trials, the authors say 67% of failures were clean tool calls with wrong values.**

Microsoft's Copilot Studio team and the data company Toloka published ThinkingBox on 3 October, a benchmark that scores AI agents on the state they leave in a database rather than on their replies. The authors say that across 121,680 trials, 67.24 per cent of failures still called tools cleanly but wrote wrong field values. No outside group has reproduced the numbers.

The write-up, posted on Hugging Face, runs 18 models against 507 business-workflow tasks inside isolated tool sessions. Each task is attempted 20 times, because the authors argue that one success is not reliability. Their stated thesis is that a tool call is not an outcome. Collaborators include researchers at the University of Pittsburgh and Northwestern University.

On a single attempt, Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 leads at 67.16 per cent, the authors report. Moonshot's Kimi-K3 solves 93.89 per cent of tasks at least once but only 13.41 per cent every time. Opus 5.5 and Opus 5 each pass 241 tasks on all 20 tries, the most consistent results in the table.

The authors also price dependability. They say GPT-5.4 has the lowest cost per dependable task at $6.80, while GPT-6 Astra reaches more consistent results, 231 tasks, at $7.45 a task. Roughly four in five failures come from tool handling rather than reasoning, according to the write-up, which suggests the weak point is plumbing, not intelligence.

The framework is MIT-licensed and the benchmark data uses the CDLA-Permissive-2.0 licence. Both are on Hugging Face as microsoft/ThinkingBox-Bench and as an OpenEnv environment under BSD-3-Clause. Because the results come from the benchmark's own authors, and model makers have not commented, the rankings should be read as one team's measurement until others rerun them.

## Willison says coding agents mean pay-per-use services need hard spending caps

**Simon Willison argues cloud services should cut off at a set bill by default, since agents make surprise bills of thousands of dollars easier. AWS and Google Cloud only just added caps.**

Developer Simon Willison argued on 3 October that pay-by-usage services and APIs should ship with default hard budget caps, a setting that stops service and returns errors once a monthly limit is reached. His post says soft caps that only send warning emails fail, because people sleep through them while bills grow.

https://x.com/simonw/status/2106528704902164855

Willison ties the proposal to coding agents. In his telling, they make it easier to deploy applications that can generate large, unexpected charges. He puts the typical damage at several hundred or several thousand dollars, with surprise bills above $10,000 the point where it becomes serious, and says some developers avoid AWS for personal projects out of fear of exactly that.

He credits two providers with moving. AWS launched spending limits on 16 September, he writes: when usage reaches the cap, the project pauses for the month, though he says the feature is limited to select customers. Google Cloud added Spend Caps in July, letting users cap spending on specific services inside a project.

Willison proposes that turning a cap off should need explicit wording: "Remove the budget cap. My application will not be shut down if I exceed the configured budget limit, and I will be responsible for subsequent charges." The post is opinion, not a vendor announcement. Neither AWS nor Google has commented, as far as the sources show.

The argument may matter most to people running agents unattended. A hard cap could turn a runaway loop into an error message instead of an invoice, but it also takes the service down, which is why Willison says it should be the default. He does not say which other providers lack such limits.
