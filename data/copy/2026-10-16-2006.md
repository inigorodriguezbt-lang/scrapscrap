## Claude traces NIST's mystery curve seeds to a 1990s random-number generator

**Anthropic says Claude worked out in under half an hour that the NIST P-256 curve seeds, unexplained since 2000, came from lrand48 seeded with a timestamp, not from a hashed passphrase.**

Anthropic says one of its internal research models has solved a puzzle that cryptographers have argued about for 25 years: where the NIST elliptic-curve seeds came from. According to a post on Anthropic's research site, the seeds behind the P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 curves were never explained when NIST adopted ECDSA in 2000, after the NSA supplied them.

Anthropic says an employee asked the model to work out how the seeds were generated, without giving it a widely repeated 2015 tip that they came from hashing a joke passphrase. That tip was wrong, the company says, and several staff had already chased it without success. Anthropic says Claude identified the method in under half an hour.

The seeds were not hash outputs, the post says. They came from the C library's lrand48 generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp. For P-256, Anthropic says the process ran 1,842 iterations, each drawing 20 bytes as a candidate seed. Claude noticed the top bit was zero in every word of four seeds, a pattern it put at about 1 in 5,810 by chance.

For the prime-curve seeds, which kept fewer bits per output, Anthropic says Claude recovered the generator state with lattice basis reduction, and each recovered state regenerated all 160 bits of its seed. Stepping the state back, the ten prime-curve seeds map to dates between May 1997 and April 1998, according to the post.

Anthropic says Claude also traced six base points to the random-number generator in Arjen Lenstra's FreeLIP library. The post says the base points for P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 do not fit that recipe, and the origin of the binary-curve seed B-163 is still unexplained. Anthropic also notes it does not know why some valid candidates were skipped.

The authors say this is not a cryptographic break or a weakness, and argue it makes a deliberate backdoor less plausible, since the seeds came from a simple generator seeded by the clock. Anthropic published Python code under CC0 to reproduce the seeds, and says a $36,864 bounty tied to the question will go to the Internet Security Research Group. No outside cryptographer has yet commented in the sources we have seen.

## Elorian's Glimpse can go back and look again at a video while it reasons

**Elorian says its first model, Glimpse, can crop, zoom and replay video frames mid-thought, and beats GPT-6 Astra on chart reading and video captioning at $0.60 per million input tokens.**

Elorian, a Palo Alto startup, released Glimpse on Friday, its first model, as a research preview available through an API. According to TechCrunch, a chat interface is waitlist-only. Elorian says Glimpse is built to return to the original image or video while it reasons, rather than converting it to tokens once and thinking only in text.

TechCrunch reports that Glimpse can crop into a region, pull particular video frames or run code to measure what it sees, which Elorian calls a visual scratch space. TestingCatalog, reporting the launch, says the model can also replay half a second of video at a higher frame rate before answering. TechCrunch notes OpenAI added a similar crop-and-zoom ability to its o-series models in 2025.

https://x.com/testingcatalog/status/2111174690337177607

On Elorian's own tests, Glimpse scored better than GPT-6 Astra on scientific chart reasoning and short-form video captioning, with competitive results elsewhere, TechCrunch reports. No specific scores or independent verification were given. TestingCatalog says the company priced a robot-arm simulation plan at $0.035 on Glimpse against $0.249 on GPT-6 Astra.

Elorian lists API pricing at $0.60 per million input tokens and $3 per million output tokens, according to TechCrunch, which puts it below mid-tier models such as Sonnet but above lightweight ones such as Haiku. The company says a small team trained Glimpse on a few hundred GPUs and that it is much smaller than the frontier models it is compared with.

Elorian, which TechCrunch says raised a $55 million seed round at a $300 million valuation with Nvidia and Jeff Dean among the investors, is aiming at companies with a lot of video, including robotics firms. CEO Andrew Dai, a former Google DeepMind researcher, told TechCrunch the longer goal is robot control. All benchmark claims are the company's, and nobody outside Elorian has published tests of Glimpse yet.

## PS5LM turns a jailbroken PlayStation 5 into a local LLM server

**A GitHub project with 270 stars says a jailbroken PS5 can serve GGUF models over the home network, running a 27B Qwen model at about 20 tokens a second.**

A developer going by cobanov has published PS5LM on GitHub, which loads a GGUF language model onto a jailbroken PlayStation 5's GPU and serves it to other devices on the local network. The repository showed 270 stars and 65 forks when we read it on Friday, and it reached the Hacker News front page with 56 points.

According to the README, PS5LM uses llama.cpp's Vulkan backend on the console's RDNA 2 GPU, with 11.4 GB of shared memory for the model and context. It exposes an OpenAI-style chat endpoint and an Anthropic-style messages endpoint, which the README says Claude Code can work against, plus a browser chat page.

The README's own speed table, measured on a PS5 Slim, lists Qwen 3.5 0.8B at 207 tokens a second and Qwen 3.5 9B at 55. A Qwen 3.8 27B model quantised to 9.2 GB runs at about 20 tokens a second with a 64k context. These are the author's figures; we have not run it.

It is not a one-click install. The console must be jailbroken and on firmware 7.00 to 13.60, with the kstuff-lite, etaHEN and ShadowMountPlus payloads loaded first. The README says the repository contains no Sony SDK files, keys, firmware or model weights, and that PS5LM is not affiliated with Sony.

The author also built a TV interface that shows requests as a chat on screen, a console page charting temperature and power, and a planner that picks the longest context that fits free memory. The project is licensed GPL-3.0-or-later. Whether the console holds up under sustained load is not covered in the README.
