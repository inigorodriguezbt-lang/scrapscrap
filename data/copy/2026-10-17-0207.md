## Gemini 4 Argon scores 97.5% on ARC-AGI-3, or 49.8% depending on setup

**ARC Prize results show Google's Gemini 4 Argon at 97.5% on ARC-AGI-3 with a vendor harness, but 49.8% in the standard one.**

Google DeepMind's Gemini 4 Argon scored 97.5 percent on ARC-AGI-3 using a Provider Adapter Harness, at a cost of $4,800, according to results from ARC Prize reported by The Decoder on 16 October. That makes Google the second lab past 95 percent, after OpenAI, whose GPT-6 Astra scored 99.9 percent.

The headline number depends on the test setup. The Provider Adapter Harness allows vendor-specific features such as retained reasoning and context compaction. In the Standard Harness, which allows only simple notes between rounds, Argon scored 49.8 percent at low reasoning, completing 42 of 55 games for $5,800, The Decoder reports.

Medium reasoning in the standard harness gave 46.3 percent. At high reasoning, Argon completed only 19 games, and ARC Prize published no score. The Decoder says API problems stopped Argon from finishing all 55 games, and that the reported figures include partial points from aborted runs.

On older tests, ARC Prize lists 91.3 percent on ARC-AGI-2 at $0.32 per task and 98.5 percent on ARC-AGI-1 at $0.16 per task. On the 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games, Argon needed 62 percent fewer actions than the human baseline, matching Astra, and equalled or beat the speedrun record in three.

Epoch AI's Capabilities Index puts Argon at 165, tied with Fable 5.1 and behind Opus 5.5 at 167 and GPT-6 Astra at 166, per The Decoder. Epoch's early estimates rank it eighth in math and sixth in software engineering. The outlet notes a similar harness dispute in July involving other labs' models.

## Junior share of Anthropic technical hires fell from 27% to 9%

**Anthropic's own economists report that junior staff were 9% of technical hires, down from 27%, as agents took on more work.**

Anthropic published a report on 16 October describing how AI has changed employment inside the company. Its authors, who are Anthropic employees, say the junior share of technical hires fell from 27 percent to 9 percent, and the junior share of technical staff fell from 21 percent to 14 percent since early 2025.

The report says agent work hours passed human work hours in March 2026. By August, interactive agents worked six hours for every human hour in employee-directed sessions, although about 80 percent of interactive Claude session time is spent idle waiting for a person. Code commits per engineer are about five times their level six months earlier.

In a within-person comparison of 192 adopters and 74 non-adopters, code output rose after adoption, and proxies for quality such as lines later removed or reverted showed no difference. Non-technical coding-agent sessions rose from two a month in August 2025 to 90 in May 2026. The authors also report that account executives who adopted agents closed more deals.

The authors flag limits themselves. Anthropic is an AI developer with unusually deep adoption, so results may not generalise. People who adopt tools may differ from those who do not, the company was also growing fast, and the junior-hiring chart is descriptive only. More code or deals does not necessarily mean better outcomes.

The data comes from internal telemetry, headcount records, surveys, calendar data and 17 interviews, partly coded by Claude. Meta-work such as building agent tools rose from 10 percent to 22 percent of interactive sessions. No outside party has reviewed the data, and the paper has only the report itself to go on.

## 91% of Anthropic staff expect a Nobel-level AI discovery by 2030

**Anthropic's survey of 1,334 staff and 10,980 US adults finds 91% of staff, against 39% of adults, expect AI to make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030.**

The Anthropic Institute published a survey on 16 October comparing what Anthropic's employees and the American public expect from AI. It found 91 percent of staff and 39 percent of US adults expect AI to make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030, according to the report.

On work, the gap is wider. More than half of staff expect AI to do at least 80 percent of their current work by 2030, against 5 percent of US workers. Average estimates were 75 percent for staff and 28 percent for other US workers. On suitable tasks, 59 percent of staff said AI cuts the time to a quarter or less, against 26 percent of adults who use AI at work.

The report also asked about the economy. Using a model-based method, it puts implied 2030 GDP growth at 17.1 percent for the median staff member and 5.4 percent for the median US adult. Workers who expect AI to do more of their work forecast higher unemployment, with those expecting 80 percent or more forecasting 12.2 percent, against a current 4.2 percent.

The samples differ. The US adults came from a Morning Consult opt-in online panel, fielded 11 to 23 August with 10,980 respondents and weighted to be nationally representative. The staff poll ran 9 to 12 August, was unweighted, and reflects personal views rather than company forecasts, the authors say.

A follow-up US sample in September, with 7,613 respondents, changed little, though the share expecting a Nobel-level discovery rose to 44 percent. The authors caution that the unemployment link is an association in forecasts and not a causal finding, and that visitors to their online scenario explorer are self-selected.

## Jev reaches 35% of Vercel's paid teams in two weeks

**Vercel says Jev, a small model from TypeSafe AI, reached 35% of its paid teams in two weeks, nearly double Sonnet 5.5's reach.**

Vercel says Jev, a lightweight model from TypeSafe AI, is the fastest-adopted model it has seen in a first two weeks. TechCrunch reported the data on 16 October. Within two weeks of its limited release on 15 September, 35 percent of paid teams on Vercel's platform were using it.

That reach is nearly double the second most popular model, Sonnet 5.5, according to the figures TechCrunch cites. The numbers come from Vercel's AI gateway, a unified API service similar to OpenRouter, so they show use among Vercel customers only, and they may undercount labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic that also sell direct.

TechCrunch describes Jev as a model that picks from a set of predetermined outputs. Limiting the outputs makes it cheaper and faster than models such as Sonnet, which suits bulk classification. Vercel itself used Jev to replace GPT-6 Luna for classifying harmful commands, the outlet reports. OpenAI and Amazon have released competing models.

The same Vercel data shows open-source models account for more than 70 percent of tokens processed through the gateway and nearly 25 percent of total spend. Spending on frontier labs has grown tenfold, but its share of overall spend has shrunk, TechCrunch reports.

This is one outlet's account of Vercel's own figures. The paper has not seen the underlying data, and TechCrunch notes it offers little hard data beyond Vercel's platform. Nobody outside Vercel has verified the adoption claim or tested Jev's accuracy on the classification jobs it is being used for.
