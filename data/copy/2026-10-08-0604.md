## Ethereum researchers urge 'bunker mode' as AI maths advances

**Ethereum researcher Justin Drake says AI could eventually break ECDSA, which guards crypto wallets, and urges a gradual move to fresh addresses. Vitalik Buterin backs the worry but says not to rush.**

Justin Drake, an Ethereum researcher, posted on Wednesday urging the industry to prepare for what he called "bunker mode", according to Cointelegraph. He argued that AI could eventually break ECDSA, the signature algorithm that protects wallets, and that elliptic curves may be especially exposed, since hashes are designed to have little algebraic structure. His concern is a possibility, not a demonstrated attack.

His recommendation is a controlled, gradual migration to fresh wallets whose public keys have never been exposed, with large and sophisticated holders moving first. Cointelegraph reports that Drake warned a rushed migration would do more harm than good. Nobody has shown a break of ECDSA by an AI system, and the article does not claim one.

Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin backed the warning, saying the industry should take the risks to cryptography from AI-accelerated maths seriously, but told readers he does not recommend moving funds immediately. Cointelegraph reports he extended the worry to lattice-based schemes, often considered quantum-resistant, which he said have a good chance of taking serious hits within about two years. He added that botched migrations have cost him more than hacks.

Dragonfly managing partner Haseeb Qureshi called the warning "a very sober call", saying the risk is conventional mathematics overturning unproven hardness assumptions rather than quantum computing. The Cointelegraph report ties the timing to OpenAI's release of hundreds of mathematical manuscripts on Tuesday, though the article itself notes readers should verify claims independently.

A second, unconfirmed thread runs alongside. On 7 October, computer scientist Scott Aaronson added to a blog post that, according to his anonymous sources, AI companies have begun testing whether their latest internal models can break important cryptographic protocols. He names no lab and gives no evidence. None of the labs has commented in the sources the paper has seen.

## NVIDIA robot model hits 95% on cup stacking where rivals failed

**NVIDIA and university researchers say Long-WAM, a robot model that remembers up to 19 seconds of video, stacked cups on a Unitree G1 in 95% of trials, while two rival models managed none in 20.**

Researchers at NVIDIA, MIT, HKU and UCSD have published Long-WAM, a framework for robot "world-action" models that keep more visual history without slowing the robot down. The paper, which topped Hugging Face's daily papers with 63 upvotes, has code on GitHub under NVlabs and a project page. Everything below is the authors' own reporting; no outside group has replicated it.

Their central finding, they write, is that "access to history is not the same as using it": longer histories help most when the underlying video model was pretrained autoregressively. On the RoboCasa GR-1 benchmark, success rises from 63.3% with no history to 78.7% with 19.2 seconds of it. On LIBERO-Long, it rises from 94.5% to 99.5% with 2.4 seconds.

The speed claim matters for real-time control. The authors report 107.4 milliseconds per action chunk on an RTX 5090, including prediction of future video, a 3.3 times speedup over BF16 eager execution. They report 3.2 to 4.1 times speedups across devices, using asynchronous execution and edge-hardware acceleration so that prediction does not delay actions.

On real robots, the paper reports 95% success on dynamic cup stacking with a Unitree G1 humanoid, where the π0.5 and Fast-WAM baselines succeeded in none of 20 trials. It also reports 81.7% average success on long-horizon tasks on a YAM arm, and 94.4% on RoboTwin 2.0, the best among the methods the authors compared.

Training was heavy: about 10,000 window-equivalent hours of robot and egocentric video, using roughly 30,720 GPU-hours on 64 H100 chips. The paper lists authors including NVIDIA's Jim Fan and MIT's Song Han. Benchmarks were chosen and run by the authors, and the page gives no independent test.
