## arXiv caps preprint submitters at 2 papers a month as volume doubles

**arXiv now allows two new submissions a month per submitter, after September brought 40,363 papers, roughly double the 20,569 of September 2024, it said.**

arXiv, the preprint server where much of the machine-learning field posts first, now limits each submitter to two new submissions per calendar month and three active ones at a time. It said so in a blog post on Thursday. The policy took effect on 1 October.

The server gave its own counts for the pressure. The site received 9,869 submissions in September 2016, 20,569 in September 2024 and 40,363 in September 2026. arXiv said the surge generated about 9,000 support tickets, and tied the rise to advanced AI tools.

Moderators have seen more thin papers of narrow scope, arXiv said, along with what it called salami papers, single works split into several smaller submissions. Thomas Dietterich, chair of arXiv's Editorial Advisory Board, said a small proportion of authors submit a large number of low-quality papers and consume a disproportionate share of moderators' time.

The post lists some exceptions. Rejected papers still count toward the monthly limit, while papers deleted before the announcement do not. Papers on hold from a previous month do not use up the new month's allowance, and the limits apply to the person who submits, not to co-authors. arXiv said the aim is to spread moderator time out and encourage authors to submit only their best work.

## Google says Starship needs about 1,800 launches to make space data centres work

**TechCrunch reports a Google white paper puts the figure near 180 launches a year for a decade, assuming 200 tonnes per flight and launch costs around $200 a kilogram by 2035.**

Google believes SpaceX's Starship would have to launch about 1,800 times over the next decade before orbital data centres become cost-effective, TechCrunch reported on Thursday. That works out to roughly 180 launches a year, assuming 200 metric tons per mission, according to a Google white paper the outlet says will be published in the peer-reviewed journal Joule.

The paper's cost case rests on a learning curve. Google projects launch costs could reach $200 per kilogram by 2035 if SpaceX keeps cutting prices by 20 percent a year, as it has since Falcon 1, TechCrunch said. Matching the cost fall seen with Falcon 9 would take about 370,000 tons of payload launched to orbit.

The analysis supports Project Suncatcher, Google's plan for orbital networks of 81 satellites flying in formation. Travis Beals, the Google executive running it, said the bandwidth and latency between TPUs matter a great deal when running a multi-rack workload. Google has not said when it expects to build the full network.

Radiation is the other open question. TechCrunch said Google's testing found error rates of about one in a million for inference operations, but that the chips remain a problem for large training runs lasting months. We have not read the white paper itself, and the figures here are as TechCrunch relayed them.

## Time reports Trump consulted Grok for hours before the Venezuela invasion

**Time cites an unnamed source saying Trump spent hours questioning Elon Musk's Grok about Venezuela in December, weeks before the US invaded on 3 January.**

President Trump consulted Elon Musk's chatbot Grok during a December 2025 meeting about whether to invade Venezuela, Time magazine reported, according to TechCrunch's account of the story. Time's source, who was not named, said Trump spent hours discussing Venezuela with the chatbot. The United States invaded on 3 January 2026.

According to the account, Grok described Nicolás Maduro as a deeply unpopular dictator and suggested Venezuelans would celebrate his removal. Trump also asked the chatbot how people in the country would respond to Maduro's capture. After the operation succeeded, the source said, Trump came to regard Grok as ingenious.

The claim rests on a single unnamed person familiar with the meeting. TechCrunch's article carries no statement from xAI, the White House or other officials, and we have not seen Time's original piece, so none of the details has been confirmed. Neither side's response is on record in the material we reviewed.

TechCrunch adds context from earlier reporting. By June 2026, it said, the Pentagon's AI chief had stated that the military used Grok during strikes on Iran. The outlet also noted that the December meeting came about seven months after Musk left his DOGE role, and it cited the Atlantic and the New York Times on Venezuelan boat strikes.

## Boston Dynamics gives Atlas a four-fingered hand with no pinky

**The new hand has 13 degrees of freedom, up from seven, and an Atlas engineer says a pinky was not worth three extra degrees of freedom of complexity.**

Boston Dynamics has replaced the seven-degree-of-freedom hand on its Atlas humanoid with a four-fingered design that has 13 degrees of freedom and no pinky, The Robot Report said on Thursday. The thumb has four degrees of freedom, and each of the three fingers has three.

Dylan Thrush, a mechanical engineer on the Atlas team, said the team judged the extra dexterity of a pinky not worth the complexity of three more degrees of freedom. Chief Product Officer Zachary Jackowski had team members tape their pinkies and ring fingers together for a day, and they concluded the robot did not need one.

The Robot Report described a hand of similar size to a large human hand, driven by direct actuation with a single actuator type and no fragile cables crossing the joints. Dense pressure tactile sensors sit on the fingertips and the palm. The hand can slide its thumb along the other fingers, make pinch grasps and tripodal grasps.

Boston Dynamics says the hand can work tools including drills, torque drivers, grinders, nail guns and welding torches. The design leans on reinforcement learning trained in simulation, and the company said backdrivable actuators let it simulate the hand closely. Boston Dynamics reports promising results moving dynamic tasks from simulation to hardware. The claim is its own and not independently tested.

## Ideogram says its 4.5 model edits one region and leaves the rest alone

**Ideogram says 4.5 changes only what a user asks it to and outputs at native 2K, at $0.008 to $0.22 an image across four quality tiers.**

Ideogram released Ideogram 4.5, an image model built for selective editing, on its platform and API on Thursday, The Decoder reported. The company says the model changes only the part of an image a user selects and leaves the rest intact, a problem that has plagued image editors, which tend to alter neighbouring areas.

Ideogram said the model only touches what the user tells it to, and that it limits artifacts, particularly after several edits in a row. It outputs at native 2K resolution. The company points to product photography, interior design, photo restoration and editing text inside images as the main uses.

Pricing runs across four quality tiers, from $0.008 to $0.22 per image, according to The Decoder. Ideogram named four partners: Picsart, Runway, Pika, Leonardo AI, as offering the model. An open-weight version is coming soon, the company said, without giving a date or licence terms in the material we reviewed.

The Decoder wrote that early testers confirm strong consistency across edits, but the article does not name them or give their results. The preservation claim is Ideogram's own. We have not run the model, and no independent comparison has been published against other editing models.

## Marketing firm says Opus 5.5 writes 'this matters' 116 times more than people

**Graphite compared 10,000 pre-ChatGPT articles with model rewrites and says Opus 5.5 favours 'dependable' and 'this matters', while GPT-6 Astra leans on 'not simply'.**

Claude Opus 5.5 uses the phrase this matters 116 times more often than human writers do, and why X matters 92 times more often, according to a study by the marketing firm Graphite, as TechCrunch reported on Thursday. The firm also found the model uses the word dependable 23 times more often.

OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra has different habits, Graphite said. It leans on corrective framing, in which constructions such as not simply X appear more than 100 times as often as in human samples. It also refers often to another dimension and hedges with phrases like may provide and can provide.

Graphite took 10,000 articles that predate ChatGPT as its human baseline. Different AI models then rewrote those articles from summaries, which the firm did to limit bias from the source text. It identified 13,000 phrases that appear at least twice as often in the AI versions as in the human ones.

TechCrunch said older tells such as the em dash have largely been trained out, while each new model version brings distinctive patterns of its own. We have not seen the study, and TechCrunch's account does not say whether Anthropic or OpenAI responded, so the multiples are Graphite's figures and untested by anyone else.
