## Anthropic says Claude Mythos 5 designed leaner quantum circuits

**Anthropic says Claude Mythos 5, working alone for 53 hours, found a quantum point-addition design with 17x fewer gates and cut a decoder's gate count about 12x against Google's earlier construction.**

Anthropic published a research write-up on Friday saying its Claude Mythos 5 model designed quantum circuits that beat the best published versions on several tasks, "in some cases by an order of magnitude or more". The work, which Anthropic says is also posted on arXiv as 2610.22367, was led by fellow Andi Gu through the company's STEM Fellows Program. No outside lab has tested the results yet.

Anthropic says Claude worked in a closed loop. It wrote circuits in Python, an automated evaluator checked each one for correctness and scored it on qubits multiplied by gates, and Claude revised its next attempt without human input between rounds. For elliptic-curve point addition, the core step in quantum discrete-logarithm algorithms, Anthropic says the run took 53 hours with minimal human involvement.

The headline figure is for that point-addition circuit. Anthropic says Claude found a way to decode branch information from the numbers themselves, so no history needs storing, then checked the identity numerically and formalised it in Lean. One resulting design uses 12% more qubits than the smallest earlier design but, Anthropic says, 17 times fewer gates.

A second result concerns the Reed-Solomon decoder used in Decoded Quantum Interferometry. Anthropic says Claude swapped the extended Euclidean algorithm for Berlekamp-Massey, which runs the same steps each round and suits a fixed gate sequence. It reports about 12 times fewer gates than Google's earlier construction and a worst-case memory reserve cut from 665 qubits to 19.

The write-up carries caveats. The decoder accepts a failure rate of roughly one run in 10,000, which Anthropic says sits within the task's error budget, measured over 403 million sample decodings. The authors also say Claude was conservative and favoured familiar approaches, and that circuit design is only one part of a fault-tolerant machine. They do not claim practical quantum computers are close.

## Postgres 19 slips a month as AI code review turns up defects

**PostgreSQL 19 now lands around 19 November, with up to 10 features pulled, after an EDB committer said LLM-assisted review found more defects than developers could fix in time.**

The PostgreSQL Global Development Group has delayed version 19 of the open source database, The Register reported on Friday. The release had been planned for late September or early October and is now expected around 19 November. Between eight and 10 significant features have been pulled and pushed to PostgreSQL 20, due next year. No other outlet has reported the delay yet.

Peter Eisentraut, a leading PostgreSQL committer and vice president at support firm EDB, told The Register in a blog post that LLM-assisted code review is at least partly responsible. He said some reverted features had serious architectural defects that would have been hard to fix during beta, and that the review tools had grown much more effective in the months before release.

The tools also found a long tail of minor edge-case bugs that developers might once have met years after launch. "Then you're staring at a list of forty defect reports," Eisentraut said, according to The Register, adding that even small fixes carry overhead and the team ran out of time. He said the reverts were not caused by vibe coding, since that technique was not available when most of the features were written.

One casualty is SQL Property Graph Queries, a feature the SQL standard added in 2023. The Register says PostgreSQL's implementation was removed over unresolved issues. Contributor Tom Lane had warned earlier that shipping it in version 19 would probably lead to post-release bugs that could not be fixed until version 20, and said he would "be willing to bet dinner" on it.

Eisentraut still expects PostgreSQL 19 to be a strong release, "very robust, after all the additional scrutiny", and thinks the effect will settle in future cycles if the tools keep improving. Highlights that remain include REPACK CONCURRENTLY, which reclaims disk space without the table lock that VACUUM FULL requires. The account of the delay's cause is Eisentraut's own and has not been independently confirmed.

## Mercor says 90% of US work hours lack good AI benchmarks

**Mercor mapped 29 benchmarks onto 18,797 US job tasks and found only 924 covered, which it says leaves about 90% of American working hours without solid AI tests.**

Mercor, a company that sells AI-training data, published a study on Friday that maps public AI benchmarks onto the US Labor Department's O*NET task list. It says 29 benchmarks cover 924 of 18,797 tasks and at least one task in 208 of 923 occupations. From that, Mercor estimates about 90% of American working hours go to tasks with little or no benchmark coverage. It reached the Hacker News front page with 51 points.

Coverage is lopsided, Mercor says. Tasks it rates as exposed to AI are about six times as likely to be covered as unexposed ones. The most important tasks within an occupation are six to ten times as likely to be covered as the least important. Six of 22 job families have no occupation with such coverage. Lawyers' tasks are well covered, while legal secretaries and court clerks rarely appear.

The method rests on an LLM judge. Mercor says the judge tagged each benchmark prompt with matching O*NET tasks, and a task counts as covered if it maps to at least five prompts. The company says it checked the tags against human expert tags on its own APEX benchmarks. Working-time shares come from other researchers' LLM-derived estimates, so the 90% figure is an estimate built on estimates.

Mercor also reports a pilot on investment banking, where it says Claude Opus 5.5 Max scored 73.3% on a simple prompt average and 70.2% when each O*NET task was weighted equally. Fewer than a third of the relevant tasks for the four pilot occupations have enough APEX coverage to count in the weighted scores, it says.

The authors call the results provisional. They say the 29-benchmark list is not a complete sample, which may bias coverage estimates downward, and that capping job families may understate coverage in heavily benchmarked fields such as software engineering. The page names no individual authors, and the analysis comes from a company that sells evaluation data, and no one outside Mercor has reviewed it.
