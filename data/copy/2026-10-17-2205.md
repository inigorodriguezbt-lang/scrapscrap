## NVIDIA's RTX Spark Windows laptops go on sale from $2,399 with up to 128 GB

**NVIDIA says RTX Spark laptops, pairing a Blackwell GPU with a Grace CPU and up to 128 GB of unified memory, are now on sale from six PC makers.**

NVIDIA announced on October 17 that RTX Spark laptops are now available, in a post on its own blog. The company says the machines combine a Blackwell RTX GPU with an NVIDIA Grace CPU in a single superchip, and offer up to 128 GB of unified memory. Prices start at $2,399, NVIDIA says. This account rests on NVIDIA's own announcement alone.

NVIDIA lists ASUS, Dell, HP, Lenovo, Microsoft and MSI as makers shipping laptops, with more systems "on the way". It describes the machines as slim, premium Windows PCs aimed at local AI, content creation and gaming. The post does not give model names, screen sizes or a per-model price list, so what each maker charges above the entry price is not yet clear.

The headline figure is up to 1 petaflop of AI compute, which NVIDIA claims for the superchip. The post does not say at what numeric precision that figure is measured, a detail that matters when comparing against other laptop chips. No independent benchmarks of the shipping laptops were cited, and the performance numbers should be read as the vendor's.

NVIDIA staged retail launch events in six cities: Berlin, Beijing, Istanbul, Seoul, San Francisco and Taipei. In Berlin, the company says Jensen Huang made a surprise appearance at a Saturn store on Alexanderplatz and gave away five signed laptops. NVIDIA also pointed developers to tested recipes and playbooks for running local AI on the hardware at build.nvidia.com/rtx-spark.

Huang is due to give the GTC Berlin keynote on Wednesday, October 21, from 11 a.m. to 1 p.m. CEST, according to the post. Whether NVIDIA will say more about RTX Spark there is not stated. The paper has separately carried a test of a 128 GB RTX Spark laptop running an agent workload.

## OpenCode's maker says a breach exposed provider API keys of 1.4% of users

**Anomaly Innovations, the company behind the OpenCode coding agent, says stored OpenAI, Anthropic and Google keys may be exposed in what it calls a distillation attack. Some users report unexpected charges.**

Anomaly Innovations, formerly SST and the company behind the OpenCode coding agent, has disclosed a security incident that it says affected roughly 1.4% of OpenCode Console users, according to Startup Fortune. The company told users that API keys for OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, stored when they linked their own provider accounts, may have been exposed. Startup Fortune is the only outlet we have read so far.

The company attributes the incident to a legacy console system that is being shut down, Startup Fortune reported, and calls it "related to a large scale distillation attack". Only users who had linked external provider keys were affected. Anomaly has not said how many accounts 1.4% represents, and says the incident is ongoing. Users were emailed on October 17, though Startup Fortune notes some sources give October 18.

Anomaly says it is working with Anthropic, OpenAI and Google on remediation, and that it cannot confirm whether the exposed keys were used maliciously. Its investigation so far has found no evidence that other data leaked, according to the company. How attackers reached the legacy system, and when the access was first detected, have not been disclosed.

Some users have reported unexpected charges on OpenAI and Anthropic bills, with one account describing totals climbing into the hundreds of dollars, Startup Fortune said. It also relayed posts claiming Google suspended a user's Cloud project and OpenAI deleted an account, which it could not verify. Co-founder Dax Raad reportedly said his own accounts were also targeted.

Startup Fortune recommends revoking and reissuing every provider key directly in each provider's dashboard, rather than through links in the email, then checking billing logs from October 17 onward and setting spending limits. Researcher Nathan Lambert publicly questioned whether "distillation" fits, since the term normally describes training on model outputs, not stealing credentials.
