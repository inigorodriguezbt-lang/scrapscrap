# 07 · Images

A newspaper runs a picture because it carries information the text cannot. It
does not run one to decorate the page.

## What we may publish

This is the constraint that shapes everything else. **Press agency photographs
are licensed, and we do not hold a licence.** Reuters, AP, AFP, Getty and
Bloomberg images are not available to us at any price we are paying, and a
newsroom that quietly republishes them is not saving money, it is accruing a
liability.

Four sources are safe:

| Source | Condition | Credit form |
|---|---|---|
| **Openly licensed** (Wikimedia Commons, Flickr CC) | Check the specific licence on the file page, not the site | `(Name/CC BY-SA 4.0)` |
| **Public domain** (US federal agencies, NASA, pre-1930 works) | Verify the work is actually federal, not a contractor's | `(NASA)` |
| **Charts we build** | From data we cite in the story | `(Chart: The Latent Times)` |
| **Official imagery released for press use** | Only where the publisher states press use is permitted | `(Company handout)` |

A handout image is not neutral. It is the subject's chosen picture of itself,
and the credit must say so — `(Nvidia handout)` tells the reader who framed the
shot.

**Everything else gets linked, not embedded.** A link to a photograph on the
publisher's own site is a citation. A copy on our server is a reproduction.

## When a photograph earns its place

Run one when it shows something the reader cannot otherwise see: the physical
object, the damage, the person at the moment that matters, the document.

Do not run one when it is a stock image of a server rack standing in for
"datacentre", a logo standing in for a company, or a stock photograph of a
keyboard standing in for "technology". A generic image adds page weight and
tells the reader nothing. **No image is better than a decorative image.**

## Captions

Two sentences, maximum.

**First sentence:** who is in the frame, what is happening — *in the present
tense* — and where. Add the date if the picture is recent enough for timing to
matter.

**Second sentence, only if needed:** why the picture matters to this story. If
the first sentence covers it, stop at one.

```
A Blackwell Ultra board on display at the company's San Jose campus on Tuesday.
Nvidia says the part is now shipping to cloud partners.
```

Rules that follow from that shape:

- Present tense for what the image shows; past tense for context in sentence two.
- `from left` for a row of people. Not `L-R`.
- Never state what cannot be seen. If the caption says a person is angry, the
  caption is editorialising.
- Never repeat the headline. A caption that restates the hed wastes the slot.
- Identify everyone shown, or say why you cannot.

## Credits

The credit sits in parentheses at the end of the caption, and never carries a
trailing period after the closing parenthesis. Format is
`(Source/Photographer)` or `(Photo by Name/Publication)`.

The credit is not optional and is not a courtesy. It is how the reader audits
where the picture came from, and it is what makes the licence claim checkable.

## Alt text

Every image carries alt text, written for a reader who cannot see it. Describe
the content, not the file: `Circuit board with eight processors mounted on a
black heatsink`, never `Image of a chip` or `Blackwell Ultra photo`.

Alt text is not the caption. The caption adds journalism; the alt text
substitutes for sight.
