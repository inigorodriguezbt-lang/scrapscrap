# 03 · Story structure

## The inverted pyramid

Information descends in order of importance. The story must remain coherent if
cut off after any paragraph — that is the test, and it is not a metaphor. Read
your draft stopping after paragraph one, then paragraph two. If a cut leaves
the reader misinformed rather than merely less informed, the order is wrong.

```
¶1  LEDE          what happened, to whom, with the number that matters
¶2  CORROBORATION who else confirms it, and what they disagree about
¶3  DETAIL        the specifics underneath the headline figure
¶4  METHOD        how the thing was measured, and by whom
¶5  STAKES        why it matters — only if it is genuinely at stake
```

**Five paragraphs is the house length.** Four is fine. Six needs a reason, and
seven is a feature, not a news story.

The extra room over a wire brief is for **reported detail, not elaboration**.
Raw counts behind a percentage, the method, who funded it, what the sources
disagree about, what nobody has confirmed. If you do not have that material,
file four paragraphs and go and get it. A padded story is worse than a short
one, and the reader can tell instantly which they are reading.

## The lede

**One sentence. 20–30 words.** It carries the event and the single most
important specific.

The lede answers *what happened*, not *what it means*:

> Nvidia said Blackwell Ultra began shipping to cloud partners on Tuesday, with
> the company claiming 2.4 times the inference throughput of the prior
> generation.

Not:

> The AI hardware landscape shifted significantly this week as a major new
> product entered the market.

The second sentence contains no information. It names nobody, quantifies
nothing, and could have been written before the event occurred. That is the
diagnostic: **if the lede could have been drafted yesterday, it is not a lede.**

### Five Ws, ruthlessly triaged

Cramming all five into one sentence produces the 45-word monster. Take the two
or three that carry this particular story and push the rest to paragraph two.
For a product launch that is usually *who* and *what*. For a funding round,
*who* and *how much*. For a regulatory action, *who* and *against whom*.

## Paragraphs

**40–70 words. One to three sentences.** News paragraphs are short because
columns are narrow and readers scan. A 90-word paragraph is a wall.

Each paragraph does one job. If paragraph two is doing corroboration *and*
stakes, it is two paragraphs, or the stakes material is cuttable — usually the
latter.

## The second paragraph

This is where corroboration lives, and it is the paragraph that separates a
newspaper from an aggregator. It answers: *who else says this, and does anyone
disagree?*

> Bloomberg's reporting adds a detail the company did not state: the part is
> priced below the previous generation. AMD said separately that MI400 begins
> sampling next quarter, without offering a throughput comparison.

Note what that does. It marks which fact came from whom, and it registers that
one party declined to provide a comparable number. Both are reporting.

## The third paragraph

Cut it unless it earns its place. It earns its place when it resolves a genuine
ambiguity the reader would otherwise carry away:

> Sampling and shipping are different stages. Nvidia is describing hardware
> reaching partners now. AMD is describing parts going out for evaluation later.

That is useful. This is not:

> The competition between the two companies is likely to intensify in the
> months ahead as the market for AI accelerators continues to grow.

Nobody learned anything. Delete it.

## Transitions

Don't. News paragraphs sit next to each other without connective throat-clearing.
`Meanwhile`, `Furthermore`, `It is worth noting that`, `Additionally` — all
deletable, all tells. If two paragraphs need glue to cohere, reorder them.
